The book that has benefited me most so far this year is *The Singapore Story: Memoirs of Lee Kuan Yew*.

Its impact was not merely teaching me more about the history of Singapore and Southeast Asia; it reshaped how I understand states, institutions, and development. Previously, I held simplistic ideological views: democracy is good, authoritarianism is bad; independence is good, colonization is bad; everyone having an opinion and participating politically should naturally yield fairness, prosperity, and progress.

Finishing Lee Kuan Yew’s memoirs made me realize vividly for the first time: ideology is merely an appealing sentiment—easy for the public to grasp and be attracted to, but incapable of building the world on its own. Whether a nation develops does not depend on the slogans it chants or the ideology it adopts; it depends on whether it possesses order, governance capacity, and an elite capable of executing long-term strategy.

Lee Kuan Yew struck me less as an ideologically driven politician and more as a leader with an engineer's mindset. It is not that he lacked convictions, but that he never let dogma replace reality. He practiced ruthless realism to resolve the existential challenges Singapore confronted.

## Democracy Does Not Automatically Yield Development

I once believed democratic systems possessed enormous advantages: clean, efficient, and protective of the majority. This is unsurprising; from ancient Greece and Rome onward, republicanism, democracy, and civic participation have served as powerful political narratives. The idea that every citizen should partake in public affairs carries innate appeal, often accepted without deep reflection.

*The Singapore Story* revealed the other side.

Singapore holds elections, but it is not a typical Western-style democracy. The People's Action Party (PAP) has governed continuously without interruption, and Lee himself never hid his skepticism toward Western parliamentary politics. What he prioritized above all was order, integrity, administrative efficiency, and national survival.

India, Malaysia, and many post-colonial states hold elections and promote democratic narratives, yet this did not automatically translate into wealth or effective governance. When impoverished societies rush into highly competitive electoral politics prematurely, ethnic tensions, religious divisions, regional patronage, and populist slogans are often amplified. Citizens possess formal channels to voice grievances, but the state apparatus itself lacks the institutional capacity to translate those demands into effective policy.

Authoritarian Singapore succeeded. It was neither a resource-rich giant nor preordained to prosper. In its early days, Singapore was impoverished, caught between Malaysia, Indonesia, and great-power rivalries—its very survival uncertain. Decades later, Singapore transformed into an affluent, clean, and remarkably efficient city-state, while surrounding nations developed far more slowly.

This punctures an unexamined assumption: democracy does not automatically produce clean governance, nor does strong-state rule inevitably breed corruption. In an underdeveloped country without basic administrative competence, social order, or enforceable anti-corruption mechanisms, democratic procedures can easily degenerate into empty rituals.

## Post-Colonial States Do Not Emerge Organically

The book also reframed how I perceive colonial independence.

We tend to treat "independence" as a finish line: colonizers depart, sovereignty is regained, and citizens begin forging their future. But the world Lee Kuan Yew describes is far messier. Many post-colonial nations were not organically formed nation-states; they were administrative units artificially drawn and partitioned by colonial powers.

Borders, demographic compositions, bureaucracies, and economic divisions often carried structural traps left behind by colonial rulers. Once colonizers left, a functioning state did not emerge by default. A country might boast a flag, a parliament, and elections, yet lack foundational state capacity.

This explains why many former colonies remain fixated on anti-colonial rhetoric. While morally resonant, such discourse rarely fosters economic development. Recounting his interactions with African leaders, Lee noted how frequently some indulged in denouncing Western "neocolonialism." Such rhetoric rallied domestic support and rationalized domestic underdevelopment, but it could not train workers, attract foreign direct investment, improve education, or build an effective civil service.

Here lies the danger of ideology: it flatters people into thinking they already possess the answers. Genuine nation-building, by contrast, is granular, tedious, and unyielding work.

## Ideology Is Not the Answer

Lee believed that in an impoverished, fragile, multi-ethnic society facing a hostile regional environment, surrendering governance entirely to competitive electoral politics is rarely optimal. Democracy requires prerequisites: baseline literacy, a stable civil service, a shared national identity, institutional checks on politicians, and a social fabric capable of absorbing electoral outcomes.

Without these foundations, electoral politics often descends into tribalism. Ballots are mobilized along ethnic, religious, and patronage lines; politicians chasing the next election cycle become reluctant to pursue painful, long-term structural reforms.

Singapore’s distinction lay in a disciplined, capable elite. The PAP exercised tight social control to steer the country along a long-term blueprint crafted by a competent few. The process was neither gentle nor romantic—at times deeply uncomfortable. Yet it steered Singapore clear of the traps that ensnared many post-colonial peers.

The Asian Tigers tell a similar story. Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan, and South Korea all experienced their pivotal economic takeoffs under varying forms of authoritarian governance. During crucial development phases, all benefited from external geopolitical backing, industrial relocation, export orientation, heavy educational investment, and decisive state capacity.

This was the central revelation for me: prosperity is not generated by democratic rhetoric. It stems from geographic positioning, international division of labor, Cold War or post-colonial security frameworks, industrial migration, and whether the domestic ruling coalition has the competence to seize those windows of opportunity.

## Lee Kuan Yew’s Engineer Mindset

Lee possessed a clinical engineer's mindset. In certain passages, one senses he treated Singapore’s populace almost like subjects in an iterative laboratory experiment.

He did not adopt an abstract theory and force the nation into it. Instead, he observed empirical outcomes. If a policy worked, he expanded it; if it failed, he adjusted course. Adopting English as the working language was framed as a practical necessity—to train the workforce, attract multinational corporations, and plug into global trade. Yet reading between the lines, it was equally an act of nation-building: it attenuated the emotional gravitational pull of mainland China across Chinese Singaporeans, detached disparate ethnic groups from ancestral ties, and forged a unified Singaporean identity.

Lee even considered the eugenic implications of demography, encouraging university-educated men and women to marry, believing such pairings would cultivate a durable intellectual elite. At the time, Chinese Singaporeans still adhered to traditional Chaoshan customs, where men frequently married less-educated women and had large families.

This exemplifies Lee's brand of realism: he cared little about whether a policy sounded palatable, focusing entirely on whether it maximized Singapore's long-term probability of survival.

## The Chinese Diaspora and Southeast Asia

Another profound theme is the relationship between Southeast Asia and the ethnic Chinese diaspora.

Observers in China often possess limited familiarity with Southeast Asia, knowing vaguely that migrants from Fujian and Guangdong settled across the region, perhaps recalling historical episodes of anti-Chinese violence. Many reduce this history to simple xenophobia or prejudice. Lee’s memoirs present a far more intricate historical reality.

Many Southeast Asian states long harbored deep anxieties over whether local Chinese were truly loyal to their host nations or remained emotionally and politically bound to China. These fears were far from abstract.

Historically, many Chinese regarded Southeast Asia merely as a temporary trading outpost rather than a permanent home; their true allegiance resided in Chaoshan or Fujian. Lee Kuan Yew’s own ancestors had returned from Southeast Asia back to the Chaoshan area before his family later settled in Singapore.

Following the founding of the People's Republic of China, revolution in Beijing triggered a regional butterfly effect. Southeast Asian governments scrutinized local Chinese communities with renewed suspicion, fearing ties to communist insurgencies and Beijing. This mutual distrust ultimately precipitated Singapore’s expulsion from the Malaysian federation. Lee repeatedly reflected in his memoirs that Singapore was an isolated Chinese island surrounded by 200 million Muslims.

This underscored for me that ethnicity, culture, and national loyalty cannot be neatly disentangled by progressive slogans. In modern theory, citizenship is civic and detached from ethnicity. In practice, that premise often behaves like an ideology—an idealized consensus that ignores human reality. Shifts in China’s domestic trajectory inevitably rippled into national security anxieties across Southeast Asia; those anxieties manifested as pressure to assimilate, restrict, or marginalize ethnic Chinese, at times escalating into tragic violence.

While Lee maintained that English was adopted primarily for workforce readiness and Western industrial integration, it served an equally vital purpose: severing ethnic ties with ancestral homelands and forging a cohesive Singaporean consciousness. It was, at its core, an existential survival strategy for a vulnerable enclave state.

## Small-State Diplomacy Without Romance

Lee Kuan Yew was also a master of realpolitik.

Singapore is too small to afford strategic miscalculations. Consequently, Lee placed no faith in abstract goodwill or international justice. He believed in the balance of power.

He persuaded the British to prolong their military presence, turned to Israel to build Singapore’s armed forces, normalized ties with Indonesia, backed China during the Sino-Vietnamese conflict, and provided logistical naval access to the United States. While seemingly contradictory on the surface, the underlying doctrine was consistent: Singapore could never permit any single regional power to establish uncontested hegemony.

Peace for a small state is not earned by being liked; it is secured when regional powers counterbalance one another. Only within a stable equilibrium do small nations retain breathing room.

This is the essence of Lee’s realism: never sacrificing strategic survival at the altar of ideology.

## Conclusion

Reading Lee Kuan Yew’s memoirs fundamentally shifted how I interpret the world through a realist lens.

Building a country relies not on uplifting slogans or grand ideology, but on cold, rigorous assessment of reality.

This parallels financial markets: the most widely circulated narratives are rarely the truest—they function much like ideologies, offering comfort to those seeking refuge from hard thinking. Only by discarding unexamined conclusions can one begin to navigate reality effectively.
